## Context

Aly Metwaly designed the operating model behind Nokia's enterprise AI program—spanning product management, engineering, and enablement across a global organization where a single misaligned decision could stall hundreds of teams.

Strategy was set. Tools were available. What was missing was the integrated system: who could approve new use cases, how teams escalated risk, and how daily practice connected back to executive metrics.

## Challenge

Enterprise AI fails in the gap between ambition and architecture.

Without explicit decision rights, every team negotiates its own rules—slow where caution is needed, reckless where guardrails matter. Without operating rhythms, governance becomes a document shelf while work diverges in the field. Without workflow integration, AI stays adjacent to real delivery instead of inside it.

The mandate was speed at scale—with controls executives could explain to audit, legal, and the board.

## Approach

Treated AI as an operating model problem: people, process, governance, and technology as one system—not a tooling project with policy attached later.

- **Decision rights:** Separated what leadership sets (risk appetite, investment priorities), what product owns (use-case fit and workflow design), and what teams execute (daily adoption and feedback).
- **Guardrails that travel:** Lightweight standards for data handling, review evidence, and escalation—clear enough to follow, minimal enough to keep moving.
- **Rhythms over documents:** Weekly adoption and risk reviews tied to the same metrics used in business reviews, so governance learned as usage grew.
- **Workflow-native integration:** AI capabilities placed where specifications, code, and handoffs already occurred—reducing the "extra step" tax that kills sustained use.

Governance became the interface between speed and risk—not paperwork blocking every bet.

The operating model converted fragmented pilots into a managed enterprise capability. Teams knew who decided, what evidence mattered, and how success was measured—freeing adoption programs to compound instead of renegotiate basics every quarter.
