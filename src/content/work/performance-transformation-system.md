## Context

Aly Metwaly led the rollout of **Perform to Win** at Nokia—a company-wide shift from episodic performance management to a high-performance culture run as an operating system, not an annual HR process.

The organization spanned global engineering, product, and business functions. Prior cycles concentrated energy in review season; day-to-day alignment, feedback, and differentiation were inconsistent across managers and regions.

## Challenge

Performance programs typically fail for structural reasons, not intent.

Annual goals go stale within weeks. Feedback arrives too late to change outcomes. Managers lack a shared playbook, so quality varies by leader—not by design. Transformation communications spike attention, then behavior reverts because nothing in the weekly operating rhythm reinforced the change.

Leadership needed performance to function like other critical systems: visible, measurable, and maintained—not announced once and assumed.

## Approach

Designed Perform to Win as an integrated operating system—roles, rhythms, and manager capability built together.

- **Continuous alignment:** Goals reviewed on a living cadence tied to business priorities, not locked in January and ignored by March.
- **Feedback as practice:** Structured manager rhythms for ongoing coaching and differentiation—clear expectations, timely input, documented progression.
- **Manager enablement at scale:** Playbooks, tooling, and leadership sponsorship so consistency did not depend on individual heroics.
- **Differentiation with clarity:** Performance outcomes connected to how teams were staffed, developed, and rewarded—making standards visible without reducing people to a single number.
- **Executive sponsorship wired in:** Leadership modeled the cadence in their own reviews so the system had authority beyond HR communications.

The objective was behavior change in how work gets directed and reviewed—sustained past launch.

Perform to Win moved performance from a calendar event to an embedded operating rhythm. Goal alignment, feedback, and differentiation became part of how managers ran teams week to week—across business and engineering lines at company scale.
